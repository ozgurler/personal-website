From Go-to-Market to Go-to-Market Engineering

The lever used to be headcount and now it is system design, which changes who succeeds in the role, what the stack looks like and which parts of the job you should refuse to automate.

For most of the last decade, scaling go-to-market meant hiring more people, so if you needed twice the pipeline you hired twice the SDRs. The playbook was linear and well understood, and it worked for a while.

I believe that equation has broken down, even though outbound itself still works, as the inputs changed underneath it. Buyers research on their own long before they talk to anyone, inbox providers tightened the rules on bulk sending, and capital got more expensive so adding headcount stopped being an automatic yes. In addition to that, a generation of tools arrived which can do the research, enrichment and drafting that a junior rep used to do manually.

What came out of it is a different discipline. The title varies, such as GTM engineer, growth engineer or RevOps architect, but the shape is the same: the person responsible for pipeline now builds systems rather than running plays. As an engineer who moved into sales, I find this shift less surprising than most people do, as it is the same transition manufacturing made decades ago and it is arriving late to the commercial side of the business.

What actually changed

Then

Scale by adding reps. Output rises roughly in proportion to headcount.

Now

Scale by improving the system. One well-built workflow serves the whole team.

Then

Static lists: pull a title-and-industry filter, work it top to bottom.

Now

Live signals such as hiring changes, funding, tech installs and leadership moves, which trigger outreach at the moment it is relevant.

Then

Personalization was a manual tax of around 15 minutes of research per prospect, so most reps skipped it.

Now

Research and drafting are automated, so the rep's time goes to judgment and conversation.

Then

Reporting looked backward: what closed last quarter.

Now

Instrumentation looks forward: which signals, segments, and messages actually convert.

Why the tools forced the change

Three technical developments did most of the work here, and they build on each other.

Data became programmable. Contact and company data used to arrive as a CSV which you bought once and watched decay. Now enrichment runs through APIs, so you can look up a company, verify an email, check funding history or detect a technology install as a step inside a workflow rather than as a purchasing decision. In practice it means your targeting can update continuously instead of quarterly.

Language models absorbed the research layer. I believe the time-consuming part of good outbound was never writing the email, as most of the hours went to reading the annual report, the job posting, the press release and the LinkedIn activity in order to find something worth saying. That work can now be automated to a large extent, which removes the old tradeoff between volume and relevance that the SDR model was built on.

Orchestration got accessible. Connecting a signal source to an enrichment step and then to a CRM update used to require an engineer. With workflow platforms and AI connectors, a commercially minded person can now assemble that chain on their own, so the bottleneck moved from "can you code it" to "do you know what to build."

The uncomfortable implication: when research and drafting cost nearly nothing, effort stops being the differentiator and judgment takes its place, which means knowing which signals matter, which segments deserve attention and what is worth saying. Volume was an advantage when it was expensive, and I doubt it is one anymore.

What the GTM engineering stack looks like

Please consider this as a set of layers rather than a product list. Most teams already own tools in each layer, so the engineering is in how they connect.

1. Signal layer

What tells you an account is worth contacting now: job postings, funding events, leadership changes, product launches, technology installs, website visits, expiring contracts.

The strategic question: which signals actually precede a purchase in your market?
2. Data & enrichment layer

Turning a signal into a workable record: verified contacts, firmographics, org structure, deliverable email addresses.

Failure mode: enriching everything. Enrichment costs money, so spend it after qualification.
3. Intelligence layer

Where AI reads context and produces drafts: research summaries, account briefs, tailored messaging, call prep, meeting recaps.

Keep a human on the send button. Always.
4. Orchestration layer

The logic connecting everything: triggers, branching, routing, sequencing, hand-offs to humans, exception handling.

This is where the actual engineering is, and where most stacks quietly break as well.
5. System of record

The CRM, kept honest. If your pipeline data is fiction, every layer above it is optimizing against noise.

Automating CRM hygiene is unglamorous and usually the highest-ROI step available.
6. Measurement layer

Attribution and experiment tracking: which signal, segment and message combination produced meetings, in addition to how many emails went out.

If you can't measure it, you've built automation, not a system.

The skill mix has shifted

The classic AE profile, which is relationship-driven, resilient and persuasive, still matters. I believe the people who are growing fastest right now pair it with a second set of skills:

  • Systems thinking: seeing pipeline as a set of connected stages with inputs, failure points, and feedback loops rather than a list of activities.
  • Data literacy: comfort with structured data, sane definitions, and enough statistical humility to know when a result is noise.
  • Automation fluency: a computer science degree is not required, but you should be able to reason about APIs, triggers and conditional logic, and to read what a workflow is doing.
  • Prompt and context design: knowing how to give an AI system the right inputs, constraints and guardrails is a core commercial skill now.
  • Experimental rigor: changing one variable, running it long enough to mean something, and being willing to kill what does not work.

This is why I think the engineer-to-seller path is worth more now than when I took it, as understanding how a system fails is different from knowing how to use one.

What you should refuse to automate

The failures I have seen in the last two years share a pattern, which is that teams automated the parts requiring judgment and kept doing the parts machines handle well. These are the lines I would draw:

  • Discovery. Understanding why a business needs something is a conversation, so AI can prepare you for it but it cannot hold it for you.
  • Pricing and commitments. Never let a generative system invent numbers or dates, as those should come from a controlled source.
  • Qualification judgment. Scoring models can suggest, but a human should decide what to pursue, as a model trained on last year's wins will repeat last year's blind spots as well.
  • The send button. Keep a human on it at least through the first few months, as reviewing drafts is how you find out what your system gets wrong.
  • Relationships. I doubt anybody was ever won over by hearing that their vendor's automation was efficient.

Where to start if this is new

It is the same advice I give on any automation project, which is to start narrow. Pick one motion, meaning a single segment, a single trigger and a single sequence, and instrument it before you optimize it so that you can tell whether your changes helped. Automate the research and drafting first, as that is where the hours go and where errors are cheapest to catch. Keep a human reviewing the output for at least a quarter, and widen it only after that.

I believe the teams getting leverage from this shift are the ones who picked a narrow motion, built it carefully, measured it honestly and expanded from something that was already working, regardless of how many tools they own.
