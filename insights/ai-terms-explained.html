The AI words people use without explaining them

Every vendor pitch comes with a vocabulary test now. Here are the terms which I believe matter when you are deciding what to buy, defined in plain language, with a note on why each one should affect your decision.

I was on the vendor side of technology sales for ten years, so I have watched these words being used in two ways, precisely by people who knew what they meant and as decoration by people who hoped you would not ask. Then I started building products on top of language models myself, such as an HR assistant which runs on a self-hosted model, and I had to learn what each term meant before I could choose one. This piece is that second education, written down.

You do not need a technical background for any of it. I tried to define each term the way I would explain it across a table, and after each one I added the practical reason it matters when you are evaluating a tool or a vendor. Please skip to whichever ones you keep hearing.

The foundations

Artificial intelligence (AI)

The broad umbrella, which covers any software that does something we would normally associate with human thinking, such as recognizing a face, understanding a sentence or deciding what to do next. It is a category rather than a specific technology, so a spam filter from 2005 is technically AI and so is the chatbot you used this morning.

Why it matters: when a vendor says their product "uses AI", it tells you almost nothing, so ask what kind it is and what it was trained to do.

Machine learning (ML)

The most common way of building AI today. Instead of a programmer writing rules ("if the email contains the word 'invoice', flag it"), you show the software thousands of examples and it works out the patterns itself, which means the software learns from data rather than being told what to do step by step.

Why it matters: as the system learned from data, its quality depends on that data, and a model trained on someone else's customers may not understand yours.

Neural network

A particular kind of machine learning system, loosely inspired by how neurons connect in a brain. It is made of layers of simple math units, each passing signals to the next. Individually they do almost nothing, but when enough layers are stacked they can recognize speech, read handwriting or write a paragraph. Nearly everything called "AI" in the news right now runs on neural networks.

Why it matters: most of the time you do not need to care about the internals. Please note that "deep learning" only means a neural network with many layers, so it is not a separate technology when you hear that phrase.

Large language model (LLM)

A very big neural network which is trained on enormous amounts of text in order to predict what words come next. It sounds trivial, but predicting text well enough requires absorbing grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and writing style. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are all built on large language models.

Why it matters: an LLM is a text engine, so it is very good at reading, summarizing, drafting and answering from documents. I believe products that treat it as a database, a calculator or a source of truth on its own tend to disappoint.

The numbers people quote

Parameters

The internal settings a model learned during training, stored as numbers. You can think of them as the knobs the training process turned in order to make the model's predictions better, so a model with 7 billion parameters has 7 billion of these and a model with 400 billion has 400 billion. More parameters generally means more capability, but more cost, more memory and slower responses as well.

Why it matters: parameter count is a rough size label and it does not tell you the quality, as a well-trained 8B model can beat a sloppy 70B one at a specific task. What you should ask is whether this model handles your use case well and what it costs to run.

Tokens

The units a language model reads and writes in. A token is roughly three-quarters of an English word, so "understanding" might be two tokens and "the" is one. Models have a limit on how many tokens they can hold in mind at once (the "context window"), and cloud providers bill by the token.

Why it matters: this is how AI services are priced and why long conversations get expensive. If a tool quotes a per-token price, a 10-page document is around 7,000 tokens each time you send it, so small numbers add up fast.

Training vs. inference

Training is the one-time process of building a model from data, which costs millions of dollars and takes weeks on specialized hardware. Inference is using the finished model to answer a question, and it happens every time you type a prompt. So training happens once at the vendor and inference happens constantly, which is the part you pay for.

Why it matters: "we trained our own model" is a big claim and it is rarely true for small vendors. Most products run inference on a model someone else trained, which is fine, but you should know whose model it is.

Open vs. closed, and the term that gets misused

Closed model (proprietary)

A model you can use but cannot inspect or download. You send your request to the vendor's servers, they run it and they send back the answer, so you never see the model itself. GPT-4, Claude and Gemini are closed.

Why it matters: they are convenient and usually the strongest performers, but your data goes to a third party, you depend on their pricing and availability, and if they discontinue the model you have to migrate.

Open weight model

A model whose parameters (the "weights") are published for anyone to download and run. You can put it on your own server and run it without an internet connection, so no data leaves your building. Llama, Mistral and Qwen are common examples. What is not included is the training data or the full recipe used to build it, so you can use the model but you could not recreate it.

Why it matters: this is the option when privacy or cost is the constraint, such as HR documents, legal files, patient records or anything else you cannot send to a cloud API. It trades some raw capability for control.

Open source model

This is where the terminology gets sloppy. Strictly speaking, "open source" means everything is published, which includes the weights, the code and the training data, under a license that lets you do anything with it. Very few models meet that bar. Most models marketed as "open source" are actually open weight, as the weights are free but the data is not shared and the license may restrict commercial use above a certain company size.

Why it matters: if a vendor says "open source", please read the license before building on it, as some "open" licenses prohibit exactly what you would want to do. The word you usually want is open weight, and the question to ask is "what does the license permit?"

The practical split: closed models make sense when you need the best possible output and your data is not sensitive. Open weight models make sense when data cannot leave your control, when volume makes per-token pricing painful or when you need to run without internet. I believe most businesses end up with both, matched to different jobs.

Terms from the product side

Prompt

Whatever you type into the model. More broadly, it also covers the instructions a product sends to the model behind the scenes on your behalf, as most AI products are a model plus a carefully written set of prompts that shape what it does.

Why it matters: a surprising number of "AI products" are a standard model with good prompts and a nice interface. I do not mean it as a criticism, but it does mean the differentiator is often the prompts and the workflow rather than the model.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

A technique for making a model answer from your documents instead of from its general training. When you ask a question, the system first searches your files for the relevant passages and then hands those passages to the model along with your question, so the model writes its answer from what it was given. This is how most "chat with your documents" tools work, including HR assistants and support bots.

Why it matters: if you want an AI tool grounded in your own policies, contracts or product manuals, RAG is what you are buying. Ask whether it cites where the answer came from, as good RAG systems do and it is how you can verify the answer instead of trusting it.

Fine-tuning

Taking an existing model and training it a little more on your own examples so that it picks up your tone, format or specialized vocabulary. It is far cheaper than training from scratch but more involved than prompting, a bit like sending the model to a short course rather than through the whole school.

Why it matters: I believe it is often oversold. For most business needs, good prompts plus RAG get you there without fine-tuning, and it is worth it when you need a very specific style at high volume.

Hallucination

When a model states something false with complete confidence. It is not lying, as it is predicting plausible text and sometimes plausible text is wrong. A model can invent a case citation, a product feature or a statistic which sounds entirely reasonable.

Why it matters: this is why I keep saying "keep a human on the send button". Any AI output going to a customer, a regulator or a contract needs a person reading it first. Systems with RAG and citations reduce the problem a lot, but nothing removes it completely.

AI agent

A model that takes actions in addition to answering, so it searches the web, reads a file, updates a record or sends an email, and then decides what to do next based on the result. "Agentic" is the adjective. The difference from a chatbot is that an agent has tools and a loop, which means it can try something, see what happened and try again.

Why it matters: agents are where most of the automation is, and where most of the risk is as well, as they act. When a vendor says "agent", ask what actions it can take, whether a human approves them and what happens when it gets something wrong.

What to actually ask a vendor

With the vocabulary in place, the questions get simple. Which model is under the hood, and is it closed or open weight? Where does my data go when I use this? Does it answer from my documents, and does it show me where the answer came from? What does it cost at my volume, in tokens or otherwise? And what can it do on its own versus what it drafts for a person to approve?

I believe a vendor who can answer those five plainly is one you can work with. If the answer is "it's proprietary" or "our AI is very advanced", they have not told you anything, and they probably know it.
